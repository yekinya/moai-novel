## 1-3 — The Edge of the Nest

They left at noon, when Kael could stand without the world tipping.

Sepha had the satchel packed before he was upright: the carbonite from his chest seam, the moonstone shards prised out of the ruined armor, the ugly doll she'd stopped asking about. The breastplate itself she left against a rock. It had done its one job.

"You're carrying everything," he said. It came out more sour than grateful.

"You're carrying nothing, on account of the no arms. So." She got her shoulder under him. "Walk."

He walked. Each step went up through his hips and out the tops of his shoulders, where the bandages were, where the arms weren't. He counted steps to keep from thinking about it, and lost count, and started again.

The country stayed quiet. Too quiet, the kind that should have had his neck up, except nothing came. No ogre, no troll, nothing off the high rocks. The closer you sat to a dragon's heart the more the land emptied around it; even the wolves had taken the hint. It was the only thing on their side, and Kael decided not to argue with it.

The plants were the problem. The moss, the pale threads in the dirt, the water in the low places — a thin green poison ran through all of it, slow and numbing, and he felt it worst in the raw ends of his arms, a creeping deadness that gnawed at the healing.

"Don't drink that," Sepha said, the second day, before he'd even reached for it. She knelt, laid a hand over the cup, and the pale light of a Karsha working ran down into the water. "Now you can."

"You check everything twice."

"I checked your shoulder once. It went grey. So." She handed him the cup and held it while he drank, because he couldn't. "Twice from now on."

The village came up sooner than either of them had braced for. He'd counted on a week of this. On the third afternoon the trees thinned, and there were fences, and a road, and the round warm reek of a place that fed itself — smoke, dung, bread. A small frontier town at the edge of the eight kingdoms, the kind that asked strangers nothing, because strangers were half of what it lived on.

The healer there had set worse than a man with no arms, and didn't flinch, and didn't bless herself, which Kael decided he liked. She took payment in moonstone shards without asking where they'd cracked. She cleaned the stumps, and two days later she strapped him into the first arms he would wear: hard wood, leather, bronze pins. Forearms that stopped in blunt blocks.

He learned what they could do by failing at it. They could prop a bowl. They could hold a strap across his shoulder. They could not lift a cup, or hold a spoon, or keep him from going over when he forgot and leaned.

"Wonderful," he said, looking at the stupid wooden ends of himself. "I have arms. They just don't do anything."

"They're temporary."

"They're useless and temporary."

"Most things that keep you alive are." She cinched a strap. He noticed she'd stopped leaving the careful arm's length she'd kept the first days, and seemed not to know she'd stopped. He didn't tell her. He'd been the thing people leaned away from his whole life. He wasn't going to talk her out of leaning closer.

## 1-4 — Word from Selaen

The village kept them two weeks, which was twelve days more than Kael wanted and about right for a man who still greyed out if he stood too fast.

Sepha worked the guild office — a frontier post that moved letters and gossip for a fee and asked nothing — and put a thin word down the channels her exiled kin still used. Alive, both of us. Where do we go. Then they waited, which was the part neither of them was built for. Kael was bad at it loudly. Sepha was bad at it quietly, which was worse to be near.

"Selaen," she said, when the answer came folded small and sealed twice. "An old monastery, up in the mountains, well past Karsha's old border. My uncle's people held it the whole war. There's a road, a code, a man who'll meet us." She read it again, the way you reread good news to make sure it doesn't change. "We can go."

"We'll go," Kael said. "By way of somewhere with a forge. I'm not walking into your uncle's house with two clubs for hands."

In the slow days he tried the one thing he'd done since he was fifteen, and couldn't, and didn't understand why. The mana came up easy enough — the well of it was as deep as ever — and he'd bring it to the tips of his fingers, and there it would sit. It wouldn't leave him. A wind blade, a skim of ice, the smallest ember of flame, all of it dying at his skin with nowhere to go.

He didn't tell her. He turned it over alone, in the dark, and the only thing he was sure of was that he didn't understand his own body anymore, and that was a new feeling, and he hated it.

"You keep flexing your hands," Sepha said one night, not looking up from the herbs.

"I don't have hands."

"You keep flexing the stumps, then. Like you're reaching for something." She tied off a bundle. "It's unsettling. Stop it or tell me."

He didn't do either. He looked at the fire instead and let her think it was the pain.

## 1-5 — The Steel of Sirvein

Sirvein was a week of bad road and Sepha's shoulder, and then it was a grey sprawl that belonged to no kingdom and traded with all of them — steel, runes, medicine, and the things bought in back rooms with no names on them.

The artisan he found worked metal and knew mana-channels, which was rare. She took the broken hilt of his old sword, the dead pieces of the breastplate, and a handful of the carbonite, and over five days she built him hands of steel: plate at the palm and fingertips, wood at the core, three joints to the wrist, and threaded through it all the faintest etched lines of a channel, so fine he could barely feel them.

They were a different animal from the wood. He could close them on a cup, slowly. Work a latch. Drag a charcoal stub and make letters a child could read. The first time he lifted a full cup to his mouth and didn't spill it, neither of them said anything, which was its own way of saying it.

A street over, all day and into the night, there was the deep flat hammering of metal on metal — dwarves in the under-galleries, beating alloy the surface forges couldn't. He never saw one, only the sound, and once a broad shadow with a hammer at the mouth of a lane, gone when he looked twice.

He found out what the steel couldn't do on the third evening, alone, when the channels in his fingers had healed enough to test.

He gathered mana. He brought it up. He reached for the wind blade he'd thrown ten thousand times, the one that had come as easy as breathing since he was a boy.

Nothing left him. Not through the wood, not through the steel, not through the fine bright lines. The mana came to the surface and stopped, like water at a capped pipe — all the pressure in the world and no way out.

He sat with that a long time.

"I'm not a mage," he said to the empty room, trying the words on. "Not a mage-swordsman either. I'm a man with no arms who used to be both."

When Sepha came in he was sitting in the dark not doing anything, which alarmed her more than if he'd been bleeding.

"What."

"Nothing leaves me," he said. "The mana. I can pull it up. It won't go out. Hasn't since the cliff." He almost laughed, and it came out wrong. "Turns out the dragon took more than the arms."

She didn't say anything cheap. She crossed the room and sat where she could see his face, and that was worse and better than words.

## 1-6 — A Blade in Iron Hands

He started training the morning the healer told him not to.

There was a dead field at the edge of town, an old temple yard gone to weeds, and he stood in it with a plain steel arm wrapped on a plain steel blade and swung until the grey came up. Sepha sat him down. He got up. He swung again.

"You'll undo a month of my work in a week."

"Then we've got a week."

The blade was wrong in his hands and he knew exactly why and could do nothing about it. He tried to feed mana into the steel the way he'd once fed it into an edge to make it bite, and it died at his fingers, locked behind the same wall as everything else.

He found the other thing by accident, the way the true things come.

He'd given up pushing the mana out. He was only holding it, useless, pooled at the fingertips where it always stopped now — and out of habit or spite he swung in that state, and the blow landed heavier. Not much. A hair. As if the arm behind it weighed more than it did.

He stopped. Did it again. Same hair.

He chased it for an hour. The mana wouldn't leave him, but it would sink, if he let it — down into the meat of him, the muscle, the cords of the neck, the soles of the feet — and where it pooled the body did more than the body should. A little faster. A little stronger. He didn't know the names of what he was waking. He only knew that when he asked his body to do this, it answered, like it had been waiting for him to ask.

Sepha watched from the wall, gone very still.

"You're not casting."

"No."

"You're drinking it. Inward." She came down off the wall. "I've never seen anyone do that. There isn't a school I was taught that has a name for it."

There was a name. She found it that night, with the quiet she usually kept for bad news, asking the way a Karsha healer asks — did anyone near you in your village ever sicken with no cause. when you reach for mana now, does it feel like it comes from inside you, or from the room. the wish to kill, when it comes — before your own choice, or after.

He didn't want to answer. The part of him that read every question as a snare wanted her gone. He answered anyway, because the woman asking had carried his arms out of a ravine, and that bought her the truth.

"I'm an antroper," he said, when there was nowhere left to stand. The word was the missing middle of a hundred scraps — a vanished branch of the Karen school, the margins of his grandfather's book, a sealed page he'd read once and not understood. "I pull mana the wrong way. There's a thing in me that wants to kill, and a thing over it built to hold it down, and the way I see the world is bent. All of it's mine. Always was."

"I know," Sepha said. Just that. Then she frowned at the air around him the way she frowned at a wound, and went quiet.

"There's a crack in it," she said. "The thing that holds the killing down. I can't break it — no working touches a seal like that. But it's already cracked. And the crack runs the way something deliberate runs. Someone wanted it to give. Someone with the right to."

She arrived somewhere she hadn't meant to go, and he watched her get there.

"My uncle," she said. "Aleyan. What he said to you. What he did in the duel. The peace he signed and paid for. That's the shape of this crack. He meant it to give."

For the first time since the war the shadow behind her face — the one with her parents' names on it — thinned. She didn't say the rest of it. He watched her not say it.

She unclasped the bracelet from her wrist. The little red stone he'd seen go blue once, the night he'd said her name and meant it.

"Take it."

He looked at it sitting on his steel fingers. "You told me what it does. It's been red since you met me."

"Less, now. When that thing in you takes the wheel, the stone goes red. So you'll know which of you is driving." She didn't quite smile. "You kept asking which of the two voices is really you. That's what the stone is for."

## 1-7 — The Gate

He went to the second forge while she was at her evening rite, and didn't tell her, and was honest enough with himself to know the not-telling was the first real choice of his new life.

The steel was good. It wasn't enough. He'd run the arithmetic on the road and it was simple and ugly: the day someone came for her and he stood a sword's length too far, a steel hand and a sharp temper wouldn't close the gap. He needed one thing that reached. One killing answer at distance, kept back for the one moment that asked for it.

The back-room smith asked less than the healer had. Kael laid it out low: the left arm, the off hand, since the sword went in the right. A barrel built into it. One shot, the loading too slow for two. A trigger he could catch with his teeth at the inner forearm, so the housing would fall open and fire.

"Heavy," the smith said, weighing the shot. "And the alloy round the chamber" — he turned a dull grey sliver — "came from under the ground. Don't ask past that."

"I won't."

He paid in the pieces of an older life. While the man worked he glanced at the bracelet. The stone was red.

He held the arm up and looked at it a while, in the orange dark. He asked it the way she'd taught him. Is this me, or the other one. The want of a hidden gun. The readiness to open a man at ten paces and call it caution. Maybe both — and not being able to tell was the most himself he'd felt in weeks. But the answer came out the same from either voice, and that let him carry it.

"If I can't keep her alive," he said, low, to no one, "it won't matter which of you wanted it."

He carried the new arm out under his sleeve. From the outside it was the twin of the other. She wouldn't see it. He'd decided she wouldn't, and he carried the weight of that out of the forge with the iron.

They went up toward Selaen on a code and a folded map, three days his body argued with the whole way, and Sepha never let her shoulder leave his side.

The road tested him twice — a pair of thieves who saw two cripples and an easy purse, a small thing with too many teeth off the rocks one dusk. Neither was the kind of thing that made a story. Bandits and vermin, not monsters; the small mercy of a country that had decided to leave them be.

But he learned to read himself in them. When the thieves stepped out he felt the fast voice come up — kill — and he looked at the bracelet before he looked at the men. Red. So he kept the blow short, broke a wrist instead of a throat, sank mana into his shoulder for the weight of it and the steel hand for the speed, and the two ran, and the road went quiet. He hadn't enjoyed holding back. He noted that, and held back anyway.

"The bracelet's earning its keep," Sepha said after, dry, cleaning a graze.

"It tells me when I'm lying to myself. Rude little thing."

"It's a family heirloom. Show some respect." But she watched him a beat longer than the joke needed.

Selaen showed itself as a notch in the high rock, a watch-line, a thread of smoke. A figure came down the path, small with distance, a hand up in the shape of the code.

Kael stopped on the ridge and opened his steel fingers slow, and closed them. In the left arm something settled shut with a small soft click only he could hear.

"I lived," he said, low. "Starts here."

Sepha checked the code and nodded once. Her wrist was bare. The red stone hung on the iron fingers of his ruined left hand, where she'd left it — and where, click by quiet click, it had started keeping them both honest.
